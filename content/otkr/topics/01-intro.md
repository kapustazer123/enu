# Topic 1. Introduction to Fundamentals of Tourist and Local History Work

> **Based on the course annotation and open sources** — not the teacher's lecture. Check key points against the lectures.

Local history work studies the past, culture and people of one particular place; heritage tourism turns this knowledge into experiences for visitors. The field links historical identity, collective memory and cultural resources, and it is interdisciplinary by nature.

## Key definitions

- **Local history work** (*kraevedenie*, Kazakh *ölketanu*) – the systematic study, collection, protection and presentation of the history, nature, culture and people of one village, town or region, by museums, schools, researchers and enthusiasts.
- **Heritage** – what a community inherits from the past, values today and wants to pass on to future generations.
- **Heritage tourism** – travel to experience places, objects and traditions that represent the stories and people of the past and present.
- **Historical identity** – a community's sense of "who we are", built on its shared past.
- **Collective memory** – the way a group remembers its past together (term of the sociologist Maurice Halbwachs); it decides which events, heroes and places are important. Places that carry such memory are called "sites of memory" (Pierre Nora).
- **Cultural resources** – sites, objects, traditions, stories and people that can be studied, protected and presented.
- **Tangible heritage** – physical heritage: immovable (buildings, monuments, archaeological sites, landscapes) and movable (artefacts, manuscripts, costumes).
- **Intangible cultural heritage (ICH)** – living practices, knowledge and skills passed from generation to generation (UNESCO Convention, 2003).

## Main points

**1. From local history to tourism – the chain**
Research (archives, fieldwork, oral history) → inventory of resources → protection → interpretation (stories, routes, exhibitions) → tourism products → income and pride that support further protection.

**2. Why local history matters for tourism**
- It is the **raw material** of tours, guide texts and museum exhibitions.
- It gives a destination a **unique identity** that cannot be copied – the basis of its brand.
- It **involves the community** as researchers, guides and storytellers.
- It supports **education** and school/youth tourism.

**3. Tangible vs. intangible heritage**

| | Tangible | Intangible |
|---|---|---|
| Form | Physical, can be touched | Living practice, exists in people |
| Main UNESCO instrument | World Heritage Convention (1972) | ICH Convention (2003) |
| How protected | Conservation of materials | Safeguarding through transmission and practice |
| Kazakhstan examples | Mausoleum of Khoja Ahmed Yasawi, Tamgaly petroglyphs | Nauryz, *aitys*, dombra *kui*, felt-making, kokpar |

The two often go together: a yurt is an object, but the skill of making it is intangible.

**4. UNESCO's five ICH domains** – oral traditions and language; performing arts; social practices, rituals and festive events; knowledge about nature and the universe; traditional craftsmanship.

**5. The community and intangible heritage**
ICH lives only while people practise it, so the community is its **bearer, transmitter (master–apprentice) and guardian**. The 2003 Convention puts communities at the centre: they identify their own heritage, take part in safeguarding and should benefit from it. If outsiders control it, ICH risks commercialisation and "frozen" stereotypes.

**6. An interdisciplinary field**
History and archaeology (facts), geography (places, routes), ethnography and cultural studies (traditions, identity), economics and management (products, finance), law (protection), marketing and IT (communication, digital tools).

## Examples

- A walking tour "From Akmolinsk to Astana" built from local history research on the 19th-century fortress and the city's later names (Tselinograd, Akmola, Astana).
- Turkistan uses the Yasawi mausoleum (UNESCO World Heritage, 2003) and its Sufi heritage as the core of its tourism identity.
- Felt-making masters run workshops for visitors: tourism brings income, the community keeps control of patterns and meanings.
