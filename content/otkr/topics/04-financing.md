# Topic 4. Funding Heritage Tourism: Strategies and Sources

> **Based on the course annotation and open sources** — not the teacher's lecture. Check key points against the lectures.

Heritage projects rarely pay for themselves at the start. Managers combine international, national, local and private money, write convincing proposals and build realistic budgets that keep the project alive after the grant ends.

## Key definitions

- **Grant** – non-repayable money for a specific project, spent according to the agreed plan and reported.
- **Subsidy** – state support that lowers costs for a business or organisation (for example part of interest or operating costs).
- **Co-financing (matching funds)** – the applicant's own or partner contribution, often required by funders; can be cash or **in-kind** (volunteer time, premises, equipment).
- **Public–private partnership (PPP)** – cooperation in which the state and a private company share investment, risk and income.
- **Revolving loan fund** – a fund that buys or lends money to save historic buildings; when a property is sold or a loan repaid, the money returns to the fund and is used again.
- **Funding proposal** – a document that explains a project and asks a funder for money.

## Main points

**1. Funding sources**

| Level | Examples of sources |
|---|---|
| International | UNESCO World Heritage Fund (international assistance for listed sites), UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage Fund, international development banks, EU and embassy cultural programmes, foundations |
| National | ministries of culture and of tourism, national development institutions, state programmes for culture and tourism |
| Local | regional and city budgets (*akimats*), local tourism funds |
| Private and civil | sponsorship, corporate social responsibility (CSR), patrons and donations, crowdfunding, NGOs |
| Earned income | tickets, guided tours, events, souvenirs, memberships, renting spaces |
| Fiscal tools | tourist taxes, tax incentives, conservation easements (in some countries) |

A strong project uses a **funding mix**, not one source.

**2. How a revolving loan fund works**
Capital from donations or grants → the fund buys an endangered building or lends at low interest → the property is resold with protective covenants requiring restoration → money returns → the fund saves the next building. Benefit: small capital saves many buildings and revitalises historic districts (now tourist attractions). Risk: unsold properties, need for skilled management.

**3. Structure of a funding proposal (mini-grant)**
1. Title and summary.
2. Problem / need and context.
3. Objectives (SMART).
4. Beneficiaries – direct (artisans, guides) and indirect (community, visitors).
5. Activities and methodology.
6. Timeline (e.g. Gantt chart).
7. Budget breakdown and co-financing.
8. Partners and team.
9. Expected results and indicators.
10. Sustainability – how the project continues after funding.
11. Risks and how to manage them.

**4. Realistic budget**
- Direct costs (staff, materials, equipment, printing, transport) and indirect costs (administration).
- Unit costs × quantity, based on real quotes.
- A small contingency reserve.
- Income forecast and break-even point for the period after the grant.
- Costs must match activities – funders check this.

**5. Working with agencies**
Read eligibility rules and priorities carefully, use the funder's own forms, meet deadlines, build partnerships (akimat, museum, NGO, university), keep evidence and report honestly. UNESCO funds usually go through national authorities, not to individuals.

## Examples

- A village felt-making workshop for tourists: grant from a local programme for equipment, in-kind premises from the akimat, crowdfunding for promotion, ticket and product sales for the future.
- Historic preservation foundations in Savannah and Charleston (USA) are well-known early users of revolving funds.
- Kazakhstan: national programmes for culture and tourism and support for small businesses (e.g. through the Damu fund) can be relevant – research current conditions for the independent work.
