# Topic 8. Designing the Heritage Tourism Product

> **Based on the course annotation and open sources** — not the teacher's lecture. Check key points against the lectures.

A heritage tourism product is not just a list of sites – it is a designed experience. Good products are holistic, immersive and place-based, built around a story, and they include local people, food and crafts as part of the package.

## Key definitions

- **Tourism product** – everything a visitor buys and experiences: attractions, route, content, services, people and atmosphere.
- **Product levels (Kotler)** – *core* benefit (e.g. connection with the past), *actual* product (the tour, guide, tickets), *augmented* product (extras: app, souvenir, after-visit content).
- **Experience economy** – idea of Pine and Gilmore (late 1990s) that customers pay for memorable experiences, not only services. Four realms: **entertainment, education, esthetic, escapist**.
- **Holistic product** – one complete, coherent experience, not separate services.
- **Immersive product** – visitors take part and feel "inside" the story.
- **Place-based product** – built on what is unique to this place and its people.
- **Narrative design** – organising the product as a story with characters, conflict and meaning.
- **Oral history** – recorded memories and testimonies of people about the past.
- **Heritage trail** – a marked route linking several heritage sites around one theme.
- **Tour package** – services sold together (transport, guiding, entry, food, accommodation, activities).

## Main points

**1. Tourism as experience**
Visitors remember emotions, not facts. A product should combine learning, beauty, fun and "escape" into another time (the four realms), and create an **emotional connection** (Topic 13).

**2. Significance of narrative**
- Turns separate stops into one connected, memorable experience.
- Gives human connection (real characters) and universal themes (faith, survival, love, freedom).
- Structures the route: introduction → development → climax → ending.
- Gives marketing value: a story is easy to sell and share.

**3. Oral history as a resource**
Adds human voices and hidden history (ordinary people, women, minorities); provides content for audio guides, podcasts, exhibitions; elders can become living narrators. Rules: informed consent, credit the narrator, check facts.

**4. Heritage trail design**
1. Inventory and select sites for one theme.
2. Write the theme statement; each stop is a "chapter".
3. Plan a realistic route: distance, time, safety, accessibility, rest points.
4. Interpretation and wayfinding: markers, maps, app or audio guide.
5. Partners along the route: cafés, guesthouses, craft shops.
6. Manage and evaluate: maintenance, capacity, visitor feedback.

**5. Product blueprint (seminar task)**

| Element | Questions to answer |
|---|---|
| Identity / theme | Which unique culture, period or hero? |
| Target segment | Who is it for? (Topic 2) |
| Route map | Stops, timing, transport |
| Content | Story, activities, food, crafts, meetings with locals |
| Price | Costs + margin; compare competitors |
| Promotion | Channel and message |

**6. Cultural and human elements**
Local guides, artisans, families, cuisine and music are the heart of the product; they also keep income in the community.

## Examples

- **Abai tour (Semey region):** each stop is a chapter of the poet's life; the tour ends with visitors reading one of his "Words of Edification".
- **Silk Road weekend:** Turkistan (Yasawi mausoleum) – Arystan Bab mausoleum – Otrar – Sauran, with a pottery workshop and a family dinner.
- **Freedom Trail in Boston (USA):** a red line on the pavement links historic sites into a self-guided trail.
- **Oral history tour in Astana:** memories of Virgin Lands settlers in Tselinograd become an audio walk.
