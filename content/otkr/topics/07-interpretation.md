# Topic 7. Interpretation and Storytelling in Heritage Tourism

Interpretation brings history to life through narrative design, sensory engagement and inclusive visitor experiences. Its goal is to turn static historical facts into lasting personal memories.

## Key definitions

- **Raw information** – static facts, dates and architectural terms. It is academic but passive.
- **Interpretation (Tilden's principle)** – an educational activity that reveals meanings and relationships through first-hand experience, not just facts.
- **Tilden's chain** – "Through interpretation, understanding; through understanding, appreciation; through appreciation, protection."
- **Eco-museum** – a community-based model of "living heritage" that keeps heritage in its original landscape and cultural setting.
- **Interpretive plan** – a planned process of choosing themes and media to present a site.

## Main points

**1. Three pillars of authentic heritage storytelling**
- **Human connection** – personal human struggles instead of abstract historical timelines.
- **Universal themes** – connecting local site history to broad global human values.
- **Provocative arc** – raising questions rather than giving dry encyclopedic answers.

**2. Guides as storytelling facilitators** – key competencies:
- From **monologue to dialogue** – encourage guest questions and critical reflection.
- **Cultural empathy and sensitivity** – handle controversial history with nuance and respect.
- **Non-verbal and spatial mastery** – control pace and use the site as a visual prop.
- Also: audience reading (adapt tone to group energy), fact veracity (ground myths in historic evidence), safety and flow (manage crowd movement).

**3. The eco-museum approach**
- **Territory over trophy cases** – heritage stays in its original place.
- **Community ownership** – local residents are the main narrators and custodians.
- **Intangible traditions** – focus on living crafts, folklore and oral history.

**4. Multi-sensory interpretation**
- Tactile and hands-on: material replicas, stone carving, touchable artifacts.
- Soundscapes: period music, ambient sounds, voice acting.
- Taste and smell: culinary tastings, recreation of historic scents.

**5. Tailoring stories for visitor segments**

| Segment | Techniques |
|---|---|
| Children and families | Gamification, treasure quests, character roleplay |
| Seniors and accessibility | Comfortable pace, clear acoustics, rest points |
| International guests | Visual symbols, multilingual apps, universal metaphors |

**6. Four steps to design an interpretive plan**
1. **Asset audit** – identify tangible artifacts and intangible narratives.
2. **Theme framing** – write a central "big idea" theme statement.
3. **Medium selection** – combine guides, signage, AR/VR and workshops.
4. **Visitor evaluation** – measure emotional resonance and retention after the visit.

**7. Paradigm shift: from traditional display to living interpretation**

| Traditional display | Living interpretation |
|---|---|
| Text-heavy plaques – low retention, reader fatigue | Narrative prompts – story hooks that create curiosity |
| "Do not touch" – visual-only barrier culture | Hands-on discovery – multi-sensory immersion |
| One-way delivery – expert lectures to visitors | Co-created meaning – visitors share personal reflections |

## How to answer in the exam

- Define interpretation and contrast it with raw information; quote Tilden's chain (exam question 7).
- Name the three storytelling pillars and explain what makes a message effective (question 31).
- Show adaptation for audiences, especially children (question 32), and the eco-museum benefits (question 35).
- Explain why guide training matters, using the guide competencies (question 38).
- Finish with the four-step interpretive plan or the "traditional vs. living" comparison.
