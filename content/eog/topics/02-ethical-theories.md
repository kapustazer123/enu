# Topic 2. Ethical theories and principles: utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, justice

> **Based on the course annotation and open sources** — not the teacher's lecture. Check key points against the lectures.

Ethical theories give different "lenses" for deciding what is right. In hospitality they help managers and staff explain and defend their decisions, especially in difficult cases.

## Key definitions
- **Consequentialism** — the rightness of an action depends on its results (consequences).
- **Utilitarianism** — the right action produces the greatest good (happiness) for the greatest number of people. Classic authors: Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill.
- **Deontology (duty ethics)** — some actions are right or wrong in themselves, regardless of results; we must follow moral duties and rules. Main author: Immanuel Kant.
- **Categorical imperative (Kant)** — act only on a rule that you could want to become a universal law; treat people always as ends, never merely as means.
- **Virtue ethics** — focuses on the character of the person: a good person develops virtues (honesty, courage, generosity, fairness). Main author: Aristotle.
- **Justice (fairness) theory** — ethics as fair distribution of benefits and burdens and equal treatment. Modern author: John Rawls ("justice as fairness", "veil of ignorance").
- **Rights approach** — every person has basic rights (privacy, safety, dignity) that must be respected.

## Main points

| Theory | Key question | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Utilitarianism | Which option gives the best result for most people? | Practical, considers all stakeholders | Can sacrifice the minority |
| Deontology | What is my duty? Which rule applies? | Protects rights, consistent | Rigid; rules can conflict |
| Virtue ethics | What would a good professional do? | Builds character and culture | Gives less concrete guidance |
| Justice | Is it fair to everyone involved? | Prevents discrimination | Hard to agree on what is "fair" |

Other useful principles:
1. **Golden Rule** — treat others as you want to be treated; found in many religions and cultures.
2. **Ethical relativism** — what is right depends on the culture. Useful for respecting differences, but dangerous if used to excuse bribery or discrimination.
3. **Ethical egoism** — acting in one's own interest; usually seen as a poor basis for service work.
4. **Stakeholder approach** — considers guests, employees, owners, suppliers, local community and environment.

In practice, good decisions often **combine** theories: check the consequences, the duties and rights, the fairness and the kind of person you want to be.

## Examples
- **Overbooking:** a utilitarian manager may argue that overbooking keeps rooms full and prices lower for most guests. A deontologist says the hotel made a promise (the reservation) and must keep it. A justice view asks whether the "walked" guest is fairly compensated.
- **Honest information:** a tour guide in Almaty knows a mountain road is closed but the client paid for that tour. Deontology says: tell the truth; virtue ethics says an honest guide would explain and offer an alternative.
- **Equal treatment:** under justice theory, a hotel should not give worse rooms to guests of a certain nationality or appearance.

## How to answer in the exam
- Define each theory and name its main author (Bentham/Mill, Kant, Aristotle, Rawls).
- Use a table or short comparison: key question, strengths, weaknesses.
- Apply at least two theories to the same hospitality case (e.g. overbooking) to show different conclusions.
- Conclude that practitioners combine theories and add the Golden Rule and stakeholder thinking.
