# Topic 9. Using Knowledge from Various Disciplines in Preparing and Conducting Excursions

A modern excursion needs an interdisciplinary approach: the guide combines knowledge from history, geography, art, psychology, pedagogy, ecology, sociology, political science and economics. The more fields the guide can connect, the deeper and more memorable the excursion becomes.

## Key definitions

- **Interdisciplinary approach** — preparing and conducting excursions with theoretical insights and practical tools from many academic and practical fields, not only logistics and storytelling.
- **Physical geography** — helps to interpret landscapes, ecosystems and spatial relationships.
- **Human geography** — explains urban planning, population movements and regional development.

## Main points

| Discipline | What it gives the guide | Lecture example |
|---|---|---|
| **History** | Research methods, periodization, source analysis, historiography; accurate interpretation in a wider context | Berlin walking tour: World Wars, Cold War, reunification |
| **Geography** | Landscapes, climate, biodiversity; how cities and neighbourhoods developed | Natural parks, UNESCO biosphere reserves; urban tours |
| **Architecture and art history** | Decoding styles, symbols, aesthetic principles, material choices | Louvre: Renaissance vs. Romanticism; Baroque church, constructivist Soviet building |
| **Pedagogy and communication** | Adapting to age, cognitive level, learning style; cognitive load, scaffolding, active learning; non-verbal communication, feedback | Theatrical tour in Edinburgh's Old Town: pauses, gestures, intonation |
| **Psychology** | Perception, memory, group behaviour, emotions; multi-sensory and emotional experiences; avoiding cognitive overload | Tours on tragic history (Holocaust, genocides): empathy, psychological safety |
| **Sociology** | Social history, migration, class, gender, urban diversity; inclusive narratives about marginalized groups | New York: immigration, ethnic neighbourhoods, identity |
| **Ecology and environmental science** | Ecosystems, climate change, conservation, responsible behaviour | Whale-watching in Iceland: marine biology, migration, conservation laws |
| **Political science and cultural studies** | Power, ideology, public memory; why some versions of history are promoted | Post-Soviet cities: changing monuments and street names |
| **Economics and business** | Regional economy, role of tourism, industries, supply chains | Culinary tours (local ingredients, trade); wine tourism (viticulture, branding, export) |

**Audience differences (pedagogy)**

- Children respond better to storytelling, visual cues and gamification.
- Adults often prefer structured explanations and discussion.

**Why it matters**

- Content becomes intellectually rigorous, not just anecdotal.
- It increases intellectual depth, emotional engagement and cultural relevance.
- It allows multifaceted narratives, critical thinking and meets diverse tourist interests.
- Today tourists want understanding and connection, so interdisciplinarity is "essential to the professional standard".

## Examples

Case studies from the lecture:

- **Amsterdam, Red Light District tour** — social tolerance plus law, ethics, gender studies and public health.
- **Robben Island, South Africa** — tours led by former political prisoners: personal testimony plus historical and political interpretation.
- **Singapore's Chinatown** — herbal medicine, Confucian philosophy, migration history and food anthropology.

## How to answer in the oral exam

- Define the interdisciplinary approach and say why one discipline is not enough.
- List the main disciplines and, for each, say in one sentence what it adds (use the table).
- Give 2–3 examples (Berlin for history, Iceland for ecology, Robben Island as a case study).
- Mention adapting to audiences (children vs. adults) as the pedagogy contribution.
- Conclude: interdisciplinary knowledge makes excursions deeper, more engaging and more professional.
